Levantine Arabic has eight personal pronouns. English "you" splits into three: one for a man, one for a woman and one for a group.

## The pronouns

| English | Levantine | Transliteration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | أنا | ana |
| you (to a man) | إنتَ | inta |
| you (to a woman) | إنتِ | inti |
| he | هوّي | huwwe |
| she | هيّي | hiyye |
| we | نحنا | ni7na |
| you (to a group) | إنتو | intu |
| they | هنّي | hinne |

You'll hear some regional variation: **i7na** for "we" in Palestine and Jordan, **humme** or **hum** for "they", and **huwwa** / **hiyya** in Syria. All are understood everywhere.

## No verb "to be" in the present

Put the pronoun next to the description. Nothing goes in between.

```ex
أنا من الشام | ana min ish-shaam | I'm from Damascus.
إنتِ تعبانة؟ | inti ta3baane? | Are you (f) tired?
هنّي بالبيت | hinne bil-beit | They're at home.
```

## You can usually drop the pronoun

The verb already shows who is doing it, so the pronoun is optional. Adding it gives emphasis.

```ex
بحب القهوة | b7ebb il-2ahwe | I love coffee.
أنا بحب القهوة، هوّي بيحب الشاي | ana b7ebb il-2ahwe, huwwe bi7ebb ish-shaay | *I* love coffee; *he* loves tea.
```

> The "you" you choose decides the form of the verb and adjective after it: **kiifak?** to a man, **kiifik?** to a woman, **kiifkon?** to a group ("how are you?").
