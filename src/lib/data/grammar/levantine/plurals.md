Arabic makes plurals in two ways: by adding an ending (a "sound" plural), or by changing the vowels inside the word (a "broken" plural).

## Sound plurals: add an ending

**-iin** is mostly for male people and occupations:

```ex
مهندس ← مهندسين | muhandis → muhandisiin | engineer → engineers
```

**-aat** is for most feminine nouns (drop the **-a/-e** first), and for many borrowed words:

```ex
سيارة ← سيارات | sayyaara → sayyaaraat | car → cars
ساعة ← ساعات | saa3a → saa3aat | hour → hours
تلفون ← تلفونات | talafoon → talafonaat | phone → phones
```

## Broken plurals: the word changes shape

Most everyday nouns have a broken plural. The root letters stay, but the vowels change.

| Singular | Plural | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| كتاب ktaab | كتب kutub | book |
| بيت beit | بيوت byuut | house |
| ولد walad | ولاد wlaad | boy, child |
| بنت binit | بنات banaat | girl |
| رجّال rijjaal | رجال rjaal | man |
| كلب kalb | كلاب klaab | dog |
| شبّاك shibbaak | شبابيك shbabiik | window |

There's no reliable way to predict a broken plural, so learn each one together with its singular.

## Collective nouns

Many foods, plants and animals have a "collective" form. Add **-a/-e** for one item and **-aat** to count several.

```ex
تفّاح | tiffaa7 | apples (in general)
تفّاحة | tiffaa7a | one apple
تلات تفّاحات | tlat tiffaa7aat | three apples
```

> Numbers change which form you use: 3 to 10 take the plural, but 11 and up go back to the singular. See [counting things](/grammar/levantine/numbers-counting).
