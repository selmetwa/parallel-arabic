Fusha numbers from 3 to 10 follow a famous rule: they take the **opposite gender** to the noun they count.

## The numbers

| | Fusha | With a masculine noun | With a feminine noun |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | واحد | waa7id | waa7ida |
| 2 | اثنان | ithnaani | ithnataani |
| 3 | ثلاثة | thalaatha | thalaath |
| 4 | أربعة | arba3a | arba3 |
| 5 | خمسة | khamsa | khams |
| 6 | ستة | sitta | sitt |
| 7 | سبعة | sab3a | sab3 |
| 8 | ثمانية | thamaaniya | thamaanin |
| 9 | تسعة | tis3a | tis3 |
| 10 | عشرة | 3ashara | 3ashr |

Zero is **sifr**.

## One and two: after the noun

**waa7id** and **ithnaani** behave like adjectives. They come after the noun and agree with it, usually only for emphasis, since the singular and dual already show the number:

```ex
كتابٌ واحدٌ | kitaabun waa7idun | one book
سيارتان اثنتان | sayyaarataani ithnataani | two cars
```

## Three to ten: opposite gender, then plural

The number comes first, takes the **opposite** gender of the noun's singular, and the noun follows in the genitive plural:

```ex
ثلاثةُ كتبٍ | thalaathatu kutubin | three books (kitaab is masculine, so the number takes ة)
ثلاثُ سياراتٍ | thalaathu sayyaaraatin | three cars (sayyaara is feminine, so the number has no ة)
خمسةُ رجالٍ | khamsatu rijaalin | five men
خمسُ نساءٍ | khamsu nisaa2in | five women
```

The gender is decided by the **singular**: **kutub** looks feminine, but **kitaab** is masculine, so it's **thalaathatu**.

> In everyday speech and in the dialects, this rule is mostly ignored. It matters most in careful writing and formal speech.
