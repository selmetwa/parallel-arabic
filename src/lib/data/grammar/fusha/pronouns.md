Modern Standard Arabic (Fusha) has more pronouns than the dialects, because it keeps separate forms for two people (the dual) and for groups of women.

## The pronouns

| English | Fusha | Transliteration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | أنا | ana |
| you (to a man) | أنتَ | anta |
| you (to a woman) | أنتِ | anti |
| he | هو | huwa |
| she | هي | hiya |
| you two | أنتما | antuma |
| they two | هما | huma |
| we | نحن | na7nu |
| you (men or mixed group) | أنتم | antum |
| you (women) | أنتنّ | antunna |
| they (men or mixed) | هم | hum |
| they (women) | هنّ | hunna |

In everyday reading you'll meet the singular and **antum**, **hum** and **na7nu** most. The dual and feminine plurals appear more in formal writing.

## No verb "to be" in the present

Put the pronoun next to the description:

```ex
أنا طالبٌ | ana taalibun | I am a student.
هي مُعلّمةٌ | hiya mu3allimatun | She is a teacher.
نحن من مصر | na7nu min misr | We are from Egypt.
```

## Pronouns are usually dropped before verbs

The verb already shows the person, so a pronoun adds emphasis:

```ex
أكتبُ رسالةً | aktubu risaalatan | I am writing a letter.
أنا أكتبُ وهو يقرأ | ana aktubu wa huwa yaqra2u | *I* am writing and *he* is reading.
```

> The endings like **-un** and **-an** are case endings. In speech and when reading aloud, they are often dropped at the end of a sentence: **ana taalib**.
